## C. Material facts

Tejumola & Sons Ltd owned premises at 3 Docemo Street and 42 Idumagbo Avenue, Lagos. After lengthy negotiations, UBA wrote on 19 April 1982 a letter headed 'Subject to Contract' offering to take a sub-lease: 15 years from the date physical possession was given, with the bank reserving the right to break at the end of the 5th and 10th years, at N215 per square metre per annum revisable every five years, payable five years in advance, and 'subject to your showing evidence of good title'. Tejumola replied the same day accepting the terms, confirmed the covenants, and requested that the bank take physical possession on 1 May 1982 with the five years' advance. The bank then raised structural doubts (the building was said to have collapsed during construction); the landlord paid for engineers, and Ove Arup & Partners confirmed the building was safe for banking use. At the bank's request the landlord carried out substantial alterations at its own cost. On 28 October 1982, after its own officers had inspected and been satisfied, the bank wrote calling off the transaction. The landlord sued for N2,000,000 special and general damages.

## D. Procedural history

Lagos High Court (Ayorinde J, judgment 23 November 1984): held that the contract commenced on 1 May 1982, rejected the bank's case that negotiations had not crystallised, and awarded N732,200 — N200,000 general and N532,200 special damages → Court of Appeal, Lagos Division (Ademola JCA, with Mohammed and Kutigi JJCA): appeal dismissed; the proposed date was not in doubt and was not rebutted → Supreme Court (SC.31/1987): appeal allowed, both judgments set aside, plaintiff's claims dismissed, N500 costs to the appellant.

## E. Issues for determination

As formulated by the parties and adopted by the Supreme Court: whether, on the pleadings and the evidence, the finding that there was an agreement that the lease should commence on 1 May 1982 can be sustained.

## F. Decision / holding

The Supreme Court allowed the appeal. There was no concluded contract. The offer had been made 'subject to contract'; the commencement date — an essential term of an agreement for a lease — had never been agreed; the landlord's letter proposing 1 May 1982 was a counter-offer which could only have been accepted by the bank actually taking physical possession, which never happened. The trial court and the Court of Appeal were both wrong to fix 1 May 1982 as the agreed commencement date. The plaintiff's claims were dismissed.

## G. Ratio decidendi

An agreement for a lease is subject to the general principles of contract: there is no binding contract until the parties are ad idem on the cardinal terms, and for a lease those terms include the parties, the property, the length of the term, the rent and the date of commencement. Where a term is left to be fixed by reference to a contingency that has not occurred, there is no agreement on that term and therefore no enforceable contract. An offer expressed to be 'subject to contract' keeps the matter in negotiation until a formal contract is drawn up and executed, and either party may walk away in the meantime.

## H. The exact legal rule

VERIFIED quotations: Agbaje JSC — 'as long as a necessary term of an agreement for a lease has not been agreed to the matter rests in negotiation and there is no concluded contract' (adopting Nesham v Selby (1872) 7 Ch App 406), and 'In sum I must hold that there is no concluded contract in this case and I must uphold the contention of Counsel for the Defendant that the transactions in the matter now before us on appeal were only steps in negotiations between the parties.' Obaseki JSC — 'What then does "subject to contract" mean? In my view, it means that until a formal contract is drawn up and executed everything is in the negotiation stage.' Agbaje JSC on acceptance — 'An acceptance is a final and unqualified expression of assent to the terms of an offer... Where in the attempt to accept an offer the offeree varies the terms of the offer or... introduces an entirely new term, it is not an acceptance and cannot result in a contract crystallizing.'

## I. Exceptions, limits and qualifications

(1) 'Subject to contract' is a rule of construction, not a statutory formula: if the parties' conduct shows a concluded agreement notwithstanding the label, the label will not save them (contrast Storer). (2) Estoppel may be available where the representee has been induced to act to his detriment — but on these facts no estoppel arose, because the landlord's expenditure was incurred in the hope of a contract, not on the faith of a representation that one existed. (3) The decision does not make a lawyer's involvement a requirement of validity; it is a comment on risk. (4) Obaseki JSC expressly doubted whether the strict outcome was satisfactory and called for legislative reform, observing that the Statute of Frauds and its Nigerian equivalents were meant to be a shield against frauds and not an instrument of frauds.

## J. Application to the subject

On the definition of an offer, Tejumola demonstrates the second limb: the letter had to specify terms with enough precision (premises, term, rent, revision, break clause, possession) for a definite promise to be extracted. But the case's real teaching, and its safest examination use, is on completeness and certainty of terms, on 'subject to contract', and on the distinction between an acceptance and a counter-offer — all of which fall squarely within the second condition for an offer set out at 2.2 of the uploaded page.

## K. Later treatment

UBN Ltd v Sax (Nig) Ltd (1994) 8 NWLR (Pt 361) 150 (SC) cites Tejumola at 686 for the proposition that where the parties have not agreed on a fundamental term there is no valid contract. Later Supreme Court and Court of Appeal decisions (for example I Bosah v Pius Oji (2002) and Efrede v Ita (2020) CA) apply the requirement that for a lease to be complete the parties, the property, the length of the term, the rent and the date of commencement must be defined. No decision overruling or doubting Tejumola was located.

## L. Examination significance

Cite it for: 'subject to contract' means the matter remains in negotiation; the essential terms of an agreement for a lease; and the requirement of certainty and completeness in a true offer. Do NOT cite it as authority for a bare definition of an offer, and do NOT say the Supreme Court held the negotiations produced a contract — the whole point is that they did not. The commonest mistake is to confuse Tejumola with Storer: in Storer there was no 'subject to contract' label and a contract was found; in Tejumola the label was present and no contract was found.

## Case prepared for exam — fast writing

**Citation and court.** United Bank for Africa Ltd v Tejumola & Sons Ltd (1988) 2 NWLR (Pt 79) 662 (SC).

**Facts.**

1. The respondent owned premises at 3 Docemo Street and 42 Idumagbo Avenue, Lagos, and negotiated to let them to the appellant bank for fifteen years.
2. The bank's letter of 19 April 1982 was headed 'Subject to Contract' and fixed the term as fifteen years from the date physical possession was given, with a break clause and five years' rent in advance.
3. The landlord accepted the terms the same day but asked the bank to take possession on 1 May 1982 — a date the bank had not proposed.
4. The bank raised structural doubts, and the landlord paid for two sets of engineers and carried out expensive alterations before the bank withdrew on 28 October 1982.
5. The landlord sued for N2,000,000; the High Court awarded N732,200 and the Court of Appeal upheld it, finding that 1 May 1982 was the agreed commencement date.
6. The Supreme Court allowed the bank's appeal, holding that the commencement date had never been agreed and that the dealings were still negotiations.

**Issue.** Whether, on the evidence, the lease was agreed to commence on 1 May 1982, so that a binding contract existed.

**Held.** No. The appeal was allowed, both lower judgments were set aside, and the landlord's claims were dismissed.

**Rule / ratio.** An offer marked 'subject to contract' keeps the matter in negotiation until a formal contract is executed, and there is no concluded agreement for a lease unless the parties, the property, the term, the rent and the date of commencement are all agreed.

**Exception or qualification.** Estoppel may bind a party who has induced the other to act to his detriment, and an unqualified 'subject to contract' label will not defeat a contract that the parties' conduct shows was actually concluded (contrast Storer).

**Why it matters.** It shows that a proposal can be perfectly detailed and still not be an offer capable of acceptance, because a cardinal term is missing and the parties have reserved their position.
