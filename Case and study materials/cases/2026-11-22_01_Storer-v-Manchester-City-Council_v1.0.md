# Case 1: Storer v Manchester City Council

**VERIFIED**

[1974] 1 WLR 1403; also reported [1974] 3 All ER 824; 73 LGR 1; (1974) 118 SJ 599

## A. Verified citation

[1974] 1 WLR 1403; also reported [1974] 3 All ER 824; 73 LGR 1; (1974) 118 SJ 599

## B. Source and textbook connection

Printed at p 23, fn 1.

The definition of an offer: an expression of willingness to contract on certain terms, made with the intention that it shall become binding as soon as it is accepted by the person to whom it is addressed.

## C. Material facts

In 1970 the defendant council, then Conservative-controlled, adopted a policy of selling council houses to sitting tenants. It instructed the town clerk to devise a simple form of agreement so that sales could take effect as early as possible, deliberately dispensing with the usual conveyancing formalities. The plaintiff, Desmond Harry Storer, was the tenant of 174 Charlestown Road, a council house. By a letter dated 9 March 1971 the town clerk wrote: 'I understand you wish to purchase your Council house and enclose the Agreement for Sale. If you will sign the Agreement and return it to me I will send you the Agreement signed on behalf of the Corporation in exchange.' The enclosed form had the purchaser, property, price, mortgage amount and monthly repayments filled in; only item 7 (the date the tenancy would cease and mortgage repayments begin) was left blank. The plaintiff signed the agreement and returned it. In May 1971 Labour took control of the council, reversed the policy, and informed him it would not proceed because contracts had not been formally exchanged. He sued for specific performance, alleging a binding contract. The county court judge found for him; the council appealed.

## D. Procedural history

County court (specific performance ordered; damages claim adjourned sine die) → Court of Appeal (appeal dismissed) → binding contract upheld. No further appeal was taken; the parallel case of Gibson went to the House of Lords in 1979 and was decided the other way.

## E. Issues for determination

Analytical summary, not a verbatim formulation: (1) whether a contract of sale had been concluded by offer and acceptance notwithstanding that the contracts had not been formally exchanged; (2) whether the council's letter and the enclosed agreement amounted to an offer capable of acceptance; and (3) whether the blank commencement item deprived the agreement of certainty.

## F. Decision / holding

Appeal dismissed. A binding contract for the sale of the house had been concluded by offer and acceptance when the plaintiff accepted the offer contained in the letter of 9 March by signing the agreement for sale and returning it to the council, notwithstanding that contracts had not been exchanged and the contract had not been signed on behalf of the council. The letter of 9 March was a sufficient note or memorandum. Specific performance was a proper remedy.

## G. Ratio decidendi

Where parties have not made their arrangement 'subject to contract', the only question is whether a contract has been concluded; and whether it has been concluded is judged objectively, from what the parties said and did, not from their uncommunicated intentions. The council's scheme was expressly designed to dispense with legal formalities, and exchange of contracts was one such formality. The council therefore became contractually bound when the tenant signed and returned the agreement, the blank commencement date being a matter of administrative detail only.

## H. The exact legal rule

VERIFIED quotation from Lord Denning MR: 'When parties arrange for a sale "subject to contract", that means, as a rule, that there is no binding contract until the contracts of sale have been formally exchanged. That is clear from Eccles v Bryant and Pollock [1948] Ch 93. But where there is no arrangement "subject to contract", the only question is whether a contract has been concluded: see Bigg v Boyd Gibbins Ltd [1971] 1 WLR 26.' Paraphrase of the objective test (the familiar sentence 'In contracts you do not look into the actual intent in a man's mind. You look at what he said and did...' is very widely reproduced, but I did not verify it against page 1408 of the report, so it is given here as a reported paraphrase rather than as a verified exact quotation).

## I. Exceptions, limits and qualifications

(1) The rule does not apply where the parties have expressly or impliedly made their dealings 'subject to contract'; in that case there is no binding contract until formal contracts are exchanged (Eccles v Bryant and Pollock [1948] Ch 93). (2) The decision turns on the particular documents and on the council's demonstrated intention to adopt a simplified procedure; it does not displace the ordinary conveyancing expectation that a sale of land is concluded on exchange. (3) An objectively evidenced intention to be bound is still required — the objective test does not convert every exchange of letters into a contract.

## J. Application to the subject

The case is the English anchor for the first of the two limbs of the textbook's definition: willingness to contract on certain terms coupled with an intention to be bound on acceptance. It supplies the objective (outward appearance) method of ascertaining that intention, which is the test a Nigerian court applies when deciding whether a proposal is a true offer or a mere invitation to treat. It is also the standard contrast to 'subject to contract' negotiation, and therefore the natural counterpoint to Tejumola.

## K. Later treatment

Gibson v Manchester City Council [1979] 1 WLR 294 (HL) is the critical later authority: the House of Lords held that a council's letter saying the council 'may be prepared to sell' was not an offer, distinguished Storer on the ground that Storer's letter evinced a present intention to be bound once the tenant performed the stipulated act, and restored the objective construction of the correspondence. Lord Diplock expressly distinguished Storer. MARK: Gibson's report itself was not opened in this search; its treatment of Storer is taken from case commentary and must be treated as PARTLY VERIFIED.

## L. Examination significance

Cite Storer for: the definition of an offer; the objective test of intention to be bound; and the proposition that a contract of sale of land may be concluded without formal exchange where the parties have not stipulated 'subject to contract'. Do NOT cite it for the proposition that exchange of contracts is generally unnecessary in Nigerian land transactions — it is an exception resting on the council's own simplified scheme. The commonest mistake is to say that the House of Lords agreed with Storer, or that Geoffrey Lane LJ dissented in Storer. Neither is right: the Court of Appeal in Storer was unanimous, and it was in the later case of Gibson that the House of Lords reversed the Court of Appeal (Lord Denning MR dissenting in the Court of Appeal in Gibson, with Geoffrey Lane LJ in the majority).

## Case prepared for exam — fast writing

**Citation and court.** Storer v Manchester City Council [1974] 1 WLR 1403 (CA, England).

**Facts.**

1. Manchester City Council, under Conservative control, decided in 1970 to sell council houses to sitting tenants on favourable terms.
2. It told the town clerk to draft a simple form of agreement so that sales would not wait on the usual legal formalities.
3. The plaintiff Storer was a tenant; the town clerk wrote on 9 March 1971 enclosing an agreement for sale already completed except the commencement date.
4. The letter promised that if Storer signed and returned it, the council would send back the counterpart signed on its behalf.
5. Storer signed and returned the agreement; Labour then took control, cancelled the scheme, and refused to complete because contracts had not been exchanged.
6. Storer sued for specific performance; the county court held there was a binding contract and the council appealed unsuccessfully.

**Issue.** Whether a binding contract of sale had been concluded by offer and acceptance even though the formal contracts had never been exchanged.

**Held.** Yes. The appeal was dismissed; a binding contract existed and specific performance was ordered.

**Rule / ratio.** Where the parties have not made their bargain 'subject to contract', the only question is whether a contract has been concluded, and that is answered objectively from their words and conduct — so the council was bound when the tenant did exactly what its own letter told him to do.

**Exception or qualification.** Where the dealings are expressly or impliedly 'subject to contract', no contract arises until formal contracts are exchanged (Eccles v Bryant and Pollock [1948] Ch 93).

**Why it matters.** It fixes the objective test of intention to be bound, which is the test that separates a true offer from an invitation to treat in Nigerian contract law.

## Mnemonics

- **Case-name cue:** STORE-er: the council had to honour what it had already STORED in the post — the tenant posted back exactly what he was asked to post.
- **Facts:** SIMPLE — Scheme (sell council houses) · Instructed town clerk · Missing only the date · Posted back by tenant · Labour cancelled · Exchange held unnecessary.
- **Rule:** NOT 'subject to contract' → NO formalities needed; OBJECTIVE words and conduct decide.
- **Trigger:** Whenever a question asks whether an offer was intended to be binding, or whether a land sale needs formal exchange.
