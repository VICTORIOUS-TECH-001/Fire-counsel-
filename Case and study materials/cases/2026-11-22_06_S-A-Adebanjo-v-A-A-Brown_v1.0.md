## C. Material facts

The plaintiff, Alfred Adeyinka Brown, owned Plots 1, 9 and 10 at Tanimeda, Surulere, Lagos, having bought them by an indenture of 16 December 1967 registered as No. 87 at page 87 in Volume 1268. The defendant, Adebanjo, a legal practitioner, owned the adjoining Plot 3 Block IV and began building in 1972. While digging the foundation he discovered that part of his structure fell on the plaintiff's Plot 10. He reported to his vendors, the administrators of Alhaji N. B. Soule, and they approached the plaintiff. An arrangement was reached: the whole of Plot 10 would be released to the defendant, and the plaintiff would be given Plot 2A Block 2 from the defendant's vendors in substitution. In furtherance of it the plaintiff removed his shed from Plot 10 to Plot 9 at the defendant's expense. The plaintiff then extended his own building onto Plot 10, which he had agreed to release. When this was pointed out, the plaintiff agreed to accept cash compensation for the portion retained by the defendant, demarcated the boundary with a tape, and directed the defendant to his family surveyor, Alaka, who prepared Plan AL9/1976 in February 1976 for a fee paid by the defendant. When later asked to name his figure, the plaintiff turned round, denied ever consenting, and sued. He issued his writ in February 1977, about five years after the trespass, claiming N2,000 damages and an injunction; he had since died and his son was substituted.

## D. Procedural history

Lagos High Court: the trial judge accepted the defendant's account, found that the plaintiff's conduct amounted to estoppel by election (applying Spencer Bower and Turner, Estoppel by Representation, 3rd edn, p 324 para 318), and gave judgment for the defendant → Court of Appeal: set aside several findings of fact, held that the defendant had reneged on the exchange arrangement and that the breakdown of negotiations was the defendant's fault, and interfered with the estoppel finding → Supreme Court: appeal allowed; the Court of Appeal was wrong to disturb findings of fact that were supported by the evidence, wrong to make out a case the plaintiff had never made, and wrong to reject the trial court's conclusion that estoppel applied.

## E. Issues for determination

Analytical summary from the judgment's numbered complaints: (1) whether the Court of Appeal was right to hold that the defendant reneged on an agreement for the exchange of plots; (2) whether it was entitled to interfere with the trial court's findings of fact; (3) whether the breakdown of negotiations was caused by the plaintiff rather than the defendant; and (4) whether estoppel by representation/election applied.

## F. Decision / holding

Appeal allowed. The Supreme Court restored the trial court's findings. The plaintiff's conduct — accepting the substitution of plots, removing his shed at the defendant's expense, demarcating the boundary and commissioning a survey to assess compensation — induced the defendant to believe he had waived his complaint and to continue building, so the plaintiff could not resile. There was no concluded contract of sale or exchange on which either party could sue, and no particular sum was ever agreed for the portion retained.

## G. Ratio decidendi

Where parties are still negotiating and no agreement has been concluded, neither side may rely on what was done in the course of the negotiations as a contract. But a party whose conduct has led the other to believe that a right has been given up or an arrangement accepted, and who has thereby induced the other to act to his detriment, is estopped from resiling from that position. The court also reaffirmed that an appellate court will not disturb findings of fact supported by the evidence absent perversity, and will not make out a case for a party that the party did not make for himself.

## H. The exact legal rule

VERIFIED from the judgment: the trial judge, upheld by the Supreme Court, concluded 'I have no doubt in my mind that all the ingredients to make the doctrine of estoppel by election applicable are present in this case.' The Supreme Court also held that 'a court should not make out a case for a party which he does not make for himself.' On the negotiations point, the Supreme Court's treatment is a paraphrase: dealings that never became a contract cannot be sued on as one, and 'if in the end the negotiations become fruitless and end without any contract or agreement ensuing, neither side to the bargain can rely on anything done by him in the course of the negotiations as estopping the other from resiling from the bargain' — that formulation appears in UBN v Sax's use of Adebanjo, not in Adebanjo itself.

## I. Exceptions, limits and qualifications

(1) Estoppel by election requires knowledge of the right, an unequivocal act or representation, and detrimental reliance; the trial judge expressly addressed each element. (2) Estoppel does not create a contract where none was made — it bars inconsistent conduct, it does not supply missing terms. (3) An appellate court may interfere with findings of fact where they are perverse, unreasonable or unsound, or where a substantial principle of law or procedure was violated.

## J. Application to the subject

On the uploaded page it is cited only as a supporting authority in footnote 4. Its genuine teaching for the conditions of an offer is negative: a long course of negotiation, part-performance and expense can generate estoppel yet still fail to generate a contract, because the parties never reached agreement on the essential terms — here, the amount of compensation for the land retained. That is the bridge to the second condition at 2.2 of the uploaded page: the proposition must be definite, certain, unequivocal and final.

## K. Later treatment

UBN Ltd v Sax (Nig) Ltd (1994) 8 NWLR (Pt 361) 150 cites Adebanjo for the proposition that the price of a parcel of land in negotiations for its sale is a fundamental term of the envisaged contract. Adebanjo is also cited in later Court of Appeal decisions on trespass, possession and estoppel (for example Okhuarobo v Aigbe; Unamka v Ugochukwu (2019); Jumbo v AMCON (2020) CA at 667-668). No decision overruling or doubting it was located.

## L. Examination significance

Cite it for: estoppel by election/representation in land negotiations, and for the proposition that inconclusive negotiations create no contract however much money has been spent. Do NOT cite it as a case that decided the price of land is a fundamental term — that is Sax's characterisation of Adebanjo, and even then the point is that the price was never agreed, not that the court laid down a rule. The commonest mistakes are to treat it as a contract-formation case holding that a contract existed, and to overlook that it is a decision on pleadings and estoppel.

## Case prepared for exam — fast writing

**Citation and court.** Adebanjo v Brown (1990) 3 NWLR (Pt 141) 661 (SC); also (1990) 6 SC 63.

**Facts.**

1. Brown owned Plots 1, 9 and 10 at Tanimeda, Surulere, Lagos; Adebanjo, a lawyer, owned the adjoining plot and began building in 1972.
2. Adebanjo's foundation encroached on Brown's Plot 10, and his vendors offered Brown Plot 2A Block 2 in exchange for the whole of Plot 10.
3. Brown accepted in conduct: he moved his shed off Plot 10 at Adebanjo's expense and let the building continue.
4. Brown then extended his own building onto Plot 10, and it was agreed he would instead be paid cash for the part he kept.
5. He demarcated the boundary, directed Adebanjo to his family surveyor, and a plan was prepared at Adebanjo's expense to value the land.
6. Brown later denied ever consenting and sued for trespass five years after the original encroachment, claiming damages and an injunction.

**Issue.** Whether, in the absence of any concluded contract, Brown's conduct estopped him from denying the arrangement he had accepted.

**Held.** The Supreme Court restored the trial court's judgment: estoppel by election applied and Brown could not resile.

**Rule / ratio.** Where negotiations never ripen into a contract, neither party can sue on what was done during them; but a party who by his conduct induces the other to believe a right has been given up, and so causes him to act to his detriment, is estopped from going back on it.

**Exception or qualification.** Estoppel bars inconsistent conduct only; it cannot create a contract or supply terms the parties never agreed, and it requires knowledge of the right plus detrimental reliance.

**Why it matters.** It shows the outer limit of negotiation: expense and part-performance may raise an estoppel without ever producing an offer and acceptance.

## Mnemonics

- **Case-name cue:** ADE-BANJO: he 'banged' the boundary line with a tape — and that very act estopped the plaintiff.
- **Facts:** SHED — Shed moved at defendant's expense · Half plot kept · Engineer surveyed · Denied consent.
- **Rule:** NO CONTRACT → NO CLAIM ON THE DEAL; but CONDUCT + DETRIMENT → ESTOPPEL.
- **Trigger:** When negotiations collapse after one side has spent money, or when a land dispute turns on acquiescence rather than agreement.
- **Recall drill:** State the three elements of estoppel by election and identify which fact in Adebanjo evidences each one.
