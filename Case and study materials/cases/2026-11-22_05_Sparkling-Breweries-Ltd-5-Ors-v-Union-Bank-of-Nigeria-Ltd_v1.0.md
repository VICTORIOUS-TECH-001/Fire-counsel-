## C. Material facts

PARTLY VERIFIED. The appellants were a group of companies (Sparkling Breweries Ltd and five others). They requested a letter of credit from the respondent bank. The bank granted the facility but later cancelled it, which broke down the appellants' contractual arrangements with third parties. The appellants sued on several grounds, including unlawful interference with their contracts with third parties, and failed at trial and on appeal. MARK: this account rests on law-firm commentary, not on the judgment text; it is set out in the secondary-source block below and must not be treated as the court's findings.

## D. Procedural history

Not established from the materials located. The appeal reached the Supreme Court as SC.113/1996 and was determined on 13 July 2001; the outcome at the lower courts and the precise orders made were not verified in this search.

## E. Issues for determination

Not established from the materials located, save that the appeal engaged (i) the requisites of the tort of unlawful interference with contractual relations and (ii) the consequences of grounds of appeal on which no issue is predicated.

## F. Decision / holding

PARTLY VERIFIED. On the authorities citing it: the Supreme Court held that damages for tortious interference cannot be granted where the defendant's act is not itself unlawful (at 560-561, per Ogbuagu JSC), and that a ground of appeal on which no issue is predicated is deemed abandoned and liable to be struck out (at 556).

## G. Ratio decidendi

For contract purposes the case is relied on for a general statement of what an offer is: an expression of readiness to contract on the terms specified by the offeror which, when accepted by the offeree, will give rise to a binding contract, acceptance being what converts the offer into a contract. MARK: the case's own ratio, on the tort of unlawful interference, is not a contract-law ratio and should not be conflated with it.

## H. The exact legal rule

Reported quotation, VERIFIED only as a reported quotation: 'An expression of readiness to contract on the terms specified by the offeror which when it is accepted by the offeree will give rise to a binding contract...' The fuller form, reproduced in later Nigerian judgments, is: 'An offer is an expression of readiness to contract on the terms specified by the offeror which if accepted by the offeree, will give rise to a binding contract. In other words, it is by acceptance that the offer is converted into a contract.'

## I. Exceptions, limits and qualifications

Not applicable to this authority in its contract aspect. For its own subject-matter, the qualification is that liability for unlawful interference requires an unlawful act; a lawful act that damages another's contract does not found the tort.

## J. Application to the subject

This is, on the balance of the evidence, the true source of the block quotation printed at 2.1 of the uploaded page. It matters for two reasons: it gives the Supreme Court's own formulation of the offer/acceptance relationship, and it exposes the misattribution in footnote 3. In an examination, the safe course is to cite Sparkling Breweries for the words and Dahiru v Kamale for the counter-offer and elements points.

## K. Later treatment

Cited repeatedly by the Supreme Court and Court of Appeal: Eke v Ogbonda (2006) SC.64/2001 at 556; David-West v Oduwole (2003); Josku Nigeria Ltd v Ijumu Local Government (on the basis on which parties can be bound); Akerele v Access Bank Plc (NICN) at 560-561 on the elements of unlawful interference. No decision overruling or doubting it was located.

## L. Examination significance

Cite it, if at all, only for the definitional formula on offer and acceptance, and preferably together with Dahiru v Kamale and Agomo. Do NOT cite it as a contract-formation case on its facts — its own subject is the economic tort of unlawful interference and appellate practice on abandoned grounds. The commonest mistake is to attribute the definition to Dahiru v Kamale, as the uploaded textbook does.

## Case prepared for exam — fast writing

**Citation and court.** Sparkling Breweries Ltd & 5 Ors v Union Bank of Nigeria Ltd (2001) 15 NWLR (Pt 737) 539 (SC), Ogbuagu JSC.

**Facts.**

1. The first appellant was Sparkling Breweries Ltd, suing with five associated companies; the respondent was Union Bank of Nigeria Ltd.
2. The appellants asked the bank for a letter of credit to fund their operations, and the bank granted the facility.
3. The bank later cancelled the facility, and the appellants' arrangements with third parties collapsed.
4. The appellants sued, alleging among other things unlawful interference with their contracts with those third parties.
5. The claim failed in the lower courts and again on appeal to the Supreme Court, because the bank's conduct was not shown to be unlawful.
6. MARK: the facts in the last three lines rest on commentary, not on the judgment text, and are unverified for examination purposes.

**Issue.** Whether the bank was liable for unlawful interference with the appellants' contracts with third parties; and, for contract purposes, what an offer and an acceptance are.

**Held.** Damages for tortious interference cannot be awarded where the act complained of is not unlawful; the appeal failed. A ground of appeal without a predicated issue is deemed abandoned.

**Rule / ratio.** An offer is an expression of readiness to contract on the terms specified by the offeror which, if accepted by the offeree, gives rise to a binding contract — it is by acceptance that an offer is converted into a contract.

**Exception or qualification.** Not applicable to the definitional proposition. In its own field, the tort requires an unlawful act.

**Why it matters.** It is the Supreme Court's own verbal formula for the offer/acceptance relationship, and the likely true source of the definition the textbook prints.

## Mnemonics

- **Case-name cue:** SPARKLING = the 'spark' that lights the contract: the offer is the spark, acceptance is the flame. But the bank's letter of credit was a spark that was put out.
- **Facts:** LC — Letter of Credit asked · Cancelled · Loss to third-party deals.
- **Rule:** READINESS + SPECIFIED TERMS + ACCEPTANCE = BINDING CONTRACT.
- **Trigger:** When you need an apex-court sentence defining offer and acceptance, or when a question involves interference with another's contract.
- **Recall drill:** Recite the definition and then state, in one sentence, why the case is a poor choice as an authority on offer and acceptance facts.
