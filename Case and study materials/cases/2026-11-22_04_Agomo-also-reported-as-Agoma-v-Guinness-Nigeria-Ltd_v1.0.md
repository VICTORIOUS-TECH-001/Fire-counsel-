## A. Verified citation

(1995) 2 NWLR (Pt 380) 672; SC.189/1992, judgment delivered 17 February 1995. The report heading on the copy read prints the appellant as 'Mrs Margaret Agoma'; the case is cited throughout the texts as 'Agomo v Guinness (Nig) Ltd'.

## B. Source and textbook connection

Printed at p 23, fn 2.

Cited with Tejumola for the proposition that an offer is a proposition clearly and precisely indicating the terms under which the offeror is willing to enter into a contract with the offeree.

## C. Material facts

The appellant was an employee of Guinness Nigeria Ltd who was laid off when the company reduced production. Her employment was terminated with effect from 14 July 1984, and on 7 July 1984 she collected her terminal benefits, which included two months' salary in lieu of notice. She later relied on an internal document (Exhibit B), a directive instructing the company's Personnel Department to recruit at least 1,000 employees, adding that most of those to be recruited would be persons laid off a few months earlier. She argued that Exhibit B was an offer made to the class of retrenched workers, that she belonged to that class, and that she had accepted it by signifying that she was ready, willing and available — thus creating a contract of employment. The company denied that any offer had been made.

## D. Procedural history

The appellant's claim failed in the lower courts and she appealed to the Supreme Court (SC.189/1992). The appeal was dismissed: Exhibit B was not an offer she could accept, and in any event she had ceased to be an employee when she took her terminal benefits.

## E. Issues for determination

Analytical summary: (1) whether Exhibit B was an offer to the class of retrenched employees capable of acceptance by the appellant; and (2) whether, having accepted her terminal benefits, the appellant could still assert a subsisting contract of employment.

## F. Decision / holding

Appeal dismissed. Exhibit B was an internal administrative directive to the Personnel Department and not an offer to the appellant or to any member of her class; it said 'most of those to be recruited' and not 'all', so she could not bring herself within it. Even if it had been an offer, it was addressed to employees, and she had ceased to be one on 7 July 1984. Her acceptance of terminal benefits including salary in lieu of notice put an end to any contract, real or imagined.

## G. Ratio decidendi

An offer is a proposition put by one person to another coupled with an intimation that the offeror is willing to be bound by that proposition; it must be communicated to the offeree and must evince a present intention to be bound. An internal instruction to a company's own department, which commits the company to nothing and is addressed to no offeree, is not an offer. An employee who accepts terminal benefits including salary in lieu of notice cannot afterwards assert a subsisting contract.

## H. The exact legal rule

VERIFIED quotation from the judgment: 'An offer is a proposition put by one person to another coupled with an intimation that he is willing to be bound to that proposition.' Paraphrase of the surrounding reasoning: in order to establish a contract, whether express or implied, there must be shown a meeting of the minds of the parties, with a definition of the contractual terms reasonably clearly made out, together with an intention to affect the legal relationship.

## I. Exceptions, limits and qualifications

(1) A general offer to the world at large, or to a defined class, can be accepted by performance by a person who knows of it — but the offer must actually be made, and Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co [1893] 1 QB 256 was distinguished because there the advertisement itself promised a reward. (2) An offer of a reward for the return of lost property is enforceable by a finder who returns the property knowing of the offer. (3) The bar on an employee who has taken terminal benefits applies to termination; it does not apply in the same way to an unlawful or void retirement (see the distinction drawn in Nigerian labour-law authority).

## J. Application to the subject

Supports the first limb of the textbook's definition (a proposition by the offeror to the offeree) and, more importantly, the requirement that the proposition be communicated and be intended to be binding. In a problem question it is the authority for asking 'to whom was this addressed, and did it commit the maker?' before asking whether it was accepted.

## K. Later treatment

Agomo is regularly cited in Nigerian labour-law and contract decisions for the definition of an offer and for the effect of accepting terminal benefits (for example Guinness (Nig) Ltd v Agoma in the Court of Appeal, per Ejiwunmi JCA, and the labour-law literature discussing the wrongful-retirement/wrongful-termination distinction). No decision overruling or doubting it was located.

## L. Examination significance

Cite it for: the definition of an offer as a proposition coupled with an intimation of willingness to be bound; and for the point that an internal directive is not an offer. Do NOT cite it for the general proposition that acceptance of terminal benefits always bars a claim — that depends on whether the termination was merely wrongful or the retirement unlawful. The commonest mistakes are the wrong part number (Pt 380, not Pt 580) and treating the case as being about offers to the world at large; it is really about the absence of any offer at all.

## Case prepared for exam — fast writing

**Citation and court.** Agomo v Guinness (Nig) Ltd (1995) 2 NWLR (Pt 380) 672 (SC) — textbook prints Pt 580.

**Facts.**

1. The appellant worked for Guinness Nigeria Ltd and was laid off when the company cut production in 1984.
2. Her employment was terminated with effect from 14 July 1984, and on 7 July 1984 she collected terminal benefits including two months' salary in lieu of notice.
3. She then relied on an internal company directive telling the Personnel Department to recruit at least 1,000 staff, most of them recently laid off.
4. She argued that the directive was an offer to the class of retrenched workers, that she was within that class, and that she accepted it by declaring herself ready and available.
5. The company denied that any offer had been made to her, and the courts found that the document was an internal instruction only.
6. The Supreme Court dismissed her appeal, holding that there was no offer to accept and that her contract had ended when she took her benefits.

**Issue.** Whether an internal directive to recruit staff amounted to an offer capable of acceptance by a retrenched employee.

**Held.** No. The directive was not an offer; the appeal was dismissed.

**Rule / ratio.** An offer is a proposition put to another person with an intimation of willingness to be bound by it, and it must be communicated to the offeree; an internal administrative instruction commits the company to nobody and is not an offer.

**Exception or qualification.** A genuine offer to the world at large, or to a defined class, can be accepted by performance by anyone who knows of it, as in Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Co.

**Why it matters.** It supplies the Nigerian Supreme Court's own definition of an offer and shows how strictly the courts test whether a proposition was ever addressed to the claimant at all.

## Mnemonics

- **Case-name cue:** AGOMO = 'A GO-MO(ment) too late': she wanted to go back, but the moment had passed when she took her benefits.
- **Facts:** LAID — Laid off · Accepted benefits · Internal directive · Declared availability.
- **Rule:** PROPOSITION + INTIMATION + COMMUNICATION = OFFER. A memo to your own staff is none of the three.
- **Trigger:** When the alleged offer is an internal memo, circular, advertisement or general announcement and you must ask 'was this addressed to and communicated to the offeree?'
- **Recall drill:** Give the definition of an offer in the words of the Supreme Court, then distinguish Carlill in one sentence.
