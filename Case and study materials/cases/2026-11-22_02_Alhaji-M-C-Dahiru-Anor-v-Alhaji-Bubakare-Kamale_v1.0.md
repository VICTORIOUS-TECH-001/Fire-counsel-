## Corrections and unresolved conflicts

The words 'An offer is an expression of readiness to contract on the terms specified by the offeror which if accepted by the offeree, will give rise to a binding contract. In other words, it is by acceptance that the offer is converted into a contract' are reported in Sparkling Breweries Ltd v Union Bank of Nigeria Ltd (2001) 15 NWLR (Pt 737) 539 per Ogbuagu JSC, and are reproduced verbatim in later judgments and texts under that citation. Because Ogbuagu JSC sat on both appeals, two readings are possible: (a) the textbook has mis-sourced a Sparkling Breweries dictum to Dahiru; or (b) Ogbuagu JSC repeated his own formula in Dahiru. Reading (a) is the more probable, because the textbook's footnote 4 already cites Sparkling Breweries as the 'also see'. RECOMMENDATION: cite Sparkling Breweries for the words, and cite Dahiru v Kamale for the proposition that a qualified acceptance is a counter-offer and that there must be offer, unqualified acceptance and consideration before a contract exists. Two further printed errors: the report begins at page 8, not page 50; and the first appellant is 'Dahiru', the respondent 'Kamale' (some sources print 'Kamala').

## A. Verified citation

## B. Source and textbook connection

Printed at p 23, fn 3 and the block quotation in the text.

An 'offer' is an expression of readiness to contract on the terms specified by the offeror which, if accepted by the offeree, will give rise to a binding contract; it is by acceptance that the offer is converted into a contract.

## C. Material facts

The litigation arose from the sale of the respondent's property at Mallamre Ward, Jimeta-Yola, Adamawa State, covered by Statutory Certificate of Occupancy No. GS/6706. The respondent (as plaintiff) pleaded that he had agreed to sell to the 1st appellant for a stated sum, had received a part-payment of N600,000 out of a N650,000 contract price, and that no valid contract had been reached. His writ, taken out on 31 May 1994 in the Adamawa State High Court (suit No. ADSY/39/94, Gwam J), sought declarations that no valid contract existed, that any transfer was void for want of the Governor's consent under the Land Use Act, that the 1st defendant had no title beyond a refund of the N600,000, delivery up of the title documents, N400,000 for trespass and a perpetual injunction. Each defendant counter-claimed; the 1st defendant sought specific performance, a declaration that he held the certificate of occupancy in equity, a declaration that only N50,000 remained outstanding, an injunction and N1,000,000 general damages, pleading that he had substantially altered his position under the contract. The respondent obtained judgment in the High Court and the appellants, having obtained leave on 16 March 1997, appealed on nine grounds.

## D. Procedural history

Adamawa State High Court, suit ADSY/39/94 (Gwam J) — judgment for the plaintiff/respondent → Court of Appeal, Jos Division, CA/J/68/97-(R) — appeal on nine grounds, leave obtained 16 March 1997; the ruling of 22 November 2000 determined a motion for leave to amend the grounds and to argue a fresh issue (whether, having received a part-payment of N600,000, the respondent's remedy was to sue for the balance rather than for nullification of the sale). The Court of Appeal refused that application, holding that an issue not raised below will not be entertained on appeal absent special circumstances → Supreme Court, SC.316/2001, 15 July 2005, allowing the substantive appeal.

## E. Issues for determination

Analytical summary; the issues were not formulated in the ruling read: (1) whether on the pleadings and evidence a valid contract of sale had been concluded; (2) whether the non-obtaining of the Governor's consent under the Land Use Act rendered the transaction void; and (3) before the Court of Appeal, whether the appellants could raise for the first time the new point about the effect of the part-payment.

## F. Decision / holding

The Court of Appeal held that a fresh issue would not be entertained where it was not canvassed below and no special circumstance was shown; the application failed and the ruling was dismissed with N2,000 costs. The Supreme Court allowed the substantive appeal in 2005. ## G. Ratio decidendi

For the proposition the case is actually used for in Nigerian practice: for a contract to exist there must be a definite offer, an unqualified acceptance of that offer and legal consideration; there must be mutuality of purpose and intention, and the two contracting parties must agree. A qualified acceptance is simply a counter-offer and does not give rise to a binding contract. MARK: this ratio is taken from the textbook and from reported Nigerian judgments citing the case, not from the full Supreme Court text, and is therefore PARTLY VERIFIED.

## H. The exact legal rule

Paraphrase only, because the exact words are the subject of the attribution conflict above. The rule as generally stated: an offer is an expression of readiness to contract on the terms specified by the offeror which, if accepted by the offeree, gives rise to a binding contract, and it is by acceptance that an offer is converted into a contract. A purported acceptance that varies the terms of the offer is a counter-offer, operates as a rejection of the original offer, and cannot itself create a contract.

## I. Exceptions, limits and qualifications

(1) A qualified, conditional or varying acceptance is not acceptance at all but a counter-offer (the mirror-image rule), which the original offeror may accept or reject. (2) An issue not raised in the court below will not ordinarily be entertained on appeal, save in the recognised special circumstances — the point must be substantial, no further evidence would be needed, and refusal must occasion a miscarriage of justice (Abinabina v Enyimadu 12 WACA 171; Shonekan v Smith (1964) 1 All NLR 168). (3) A disposition of land requires the Governor's consent under the Land Use Act, and want of consent is a distinct ground of invalidity from want of agreement.

## J. Application to the subject

Footnoted to the definition of an offer, and also the source of the textbook's statement that acceptance converts an offer into a contract. In problem questions it is the authority for testing whether a reply is an acceptance or a counter-offer, and for insisting that offer and acceptance be shown as separate, provable acts rather than inferred from a course of dealing.

## K. Later treatment

The case is routinely cited by the Court of Appeal and Supreme Court for the elements of a binding contract and for the proposition that a qualified acceptance is a counter-offer. Reported examples located in this search include Agu v Okpoko (2009) and Okoli v NSITF Management Board (2016), both of which cite 'Dahiru v Kamale (2005) 9 NWLR (Pt 929) 8'. No case overruling or doubting it was located.

## Case prepared for exam — fast writing

**Citation and court.** Alhaji M. C. Dahiru v Alhaji Bubakare Kamale (2005) 9 NWLR (Pt 929) 8 (SC) — textbook prints 50.

**Facts.**

1. The respondent owned land at Mallamre Ward, Jimeta-Yola, Adamawa State, under a statutory certificate of occupancy.
2. He agreed to sell it to the 1st appellant for N650,000 and received N600,000 as part-payment.
3. He then sued in the Adamawa State High Court, asking for a declaration that no valid contract existed and that any transfer was void for want of the Governor's consent.
4. He also sought delivery up of the title documents, damages for trespass and an injunction; the appellants counter-claimed specific performance.
5. The High Court gave judgment for the respondent and the appellants appealed on nine grounds after obtaining leave.
6. The Court of Appeal refused leave to argue a new point about the effect of the part-payment, holding that fresh issues are not entertained on appeal absent special circumstances.

**Issue.** Whether a valid contract had been concluded between the parties, and whether a party may raise on appeal a point never canvassed at the trial court.

**Held.** The Court of Appeal refused the fresh issue; the Supreme Court allowed the substantive appeal in 2005.

**Rule / ratio.** A contract requires a definite offer, an unqualified acceptance and legal consideration, with mutuality of purpose; a qualified acceptance is a counter-offer and creates no contract.

**Exception or qualification.** A fresh point of law may be allowed on appeal where it is substantial, no further evidence is needed, and refusing it would occasion a miscarriage of justice.

**Why it matters.** It is the Nigerian Supreme Court anchor for the counter-offer rule and for the list of elements that must coexist before a contract exists.

## Mnemonics

- **Case-name cue:** DAHIRU = 'DAre Him RUle': dare the offeree to vary the terms and he has RUled himself out by counter-offer.
- **Facts:** SALE — Sold at N650,000 · Advance of N600,000 paid · Land Use Act consent missing · Everything counter-claimed.
- **Rule:** O-A-C: Offer + unqualified Acceptance + Consideration = Contract. Vary the acceptance and you have C-O, not A.
- **Trigger:** Any time a reply to an offer adds, subtracts or alters a term, or a question asks what must co-exist for a contract to exist.
- **Recall drill:** State the citation correctly from memory (Pt 929 at page 8, not 50), then state the counter-offer rule in one sentence and give one exception about fresh issues on appeal.
